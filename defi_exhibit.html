Vote-escrow models
Governance + active yield from protocol fees
CRV BAL
Harder case
 veCRV and veBAL locking creates enhanced governance power and yield from protocol fees. The yield component is difficult to characterize as “supply and demand dynamics” — it resembles a return derived from the protocol’s economic activity, directed by governance. Whether collective DAO governance decisions aggregate into “efforts of others” under Howey is an unresolved question the release does not directly address. The release’s requirement that digital commodities lack “rights to future income, profits, or assets” is directly implicated when yield distributions are active. p.14–16; §IV.A p.24–27 —

Yield infrastructure
Managed strategies; yield-splitting and PT/YT structures
Yearn Mellow Pendle
Fact-intensive
Pendle YT: likely not a digital commodity
 Yearn: vault strategists design and manage yield-generating strategies, selecting deployment targets and optimizing returns — potentially “essential managerial efforts” under Howey §IV.A. Mellow: restaking/vault layer with curated strategy selection raises similar questions. Pendle: the PENDLE governance token may be a digital commodity (functional system, governance utility). But the analysis is different by token type: a YT (Yield Token) is definitionally a right to future yield — exactly the “rights to future income, profits, or assets” that digital commodities are required to lack. This is not merely fact-intensive for YT; the direction is strongly against digital commodity classification. A PT (Principal Token) presents a different, more ambiguous analysis. p.14 (no “intrinsic economic properties… generating a passive yield… rights to future income”); §IV.A p.24–27 Revised
Liquid staking
Staking Receipt Tokens; 1:1 backed by deposited digital commodity
Lido (stETH) Rocket Pool (rETH) cbETH
Staking Receipt Token
Not a security if conditions met (§V.B.4)
 The release provides detailed analysis for liquid staking in §V.B.4. A Staking Receipt Token that is a receipt for a non-security crypto asset (e.g., ETH) not subject to an investment contract is itself not a security. Required conditions: (1) issued 1:1 to deposited digital commodities; (2) deposited assets locked, not rehypothecated, lent, or pledged; (3) Liquid Staking Provider acts as agent — does not decide whether, when, or how much to stake; (4) rewards not guaranteed or fixed by the provider; (5) provider’s only discretionary act, if any, is selection of a third-party Node Operator. If a Liquid Staking Provider deviates from these conditions, the arrangement falls outside the release’s safe harbor. Governance token (LDO for Lido, RPL for Rocket Pool) is a separate analysis: digital commodity if functional system + governance utility + no passive yield. Note: if the deposited digital commodity is itself subject to an investment contract, the Staking Receipt Token is a security. §V.B.4 p.52–53. §V.B.4 p.52–54; §V.B.3 p.47–52; §V.B.2 p.46–47 New
Restaking
Staked digital commodities reused on additional crypto systems
EigenLayer EtherFi Symbiotic Karak
Explicit carve-out
 The release expressly declines to address restaking: “This release does not address ‘restaking,’ which is a process that allows digital commodities staked on their associated crypto network to be used on additional crypto systems.” fn.107.

No interpretive guidance is provided for restaked assets, restaking receipt tokens (e.g., eETH, weETH), or restaking protocol governance tokens. Builders and holders in the restaking ecosystem should not assume that the Protocol Staking analysis in §V.B extends to restaking arrangements — the Commission explicitly excluded it. Further agency guidance is required before any conclusions can be drawn about the securities status of restaking-related instruments. 		§V.B fn.107 (explicit carve-out; no further guidance provided) New
DEX aggregators & routing infrastructure
Governance over routing, fees, and solver incentives
1inch CoW Protocol Kyberswap Jumper
Digital commodity
Conditional — see analysis
 Token utility is typically governance over routing parameters, fee structures, or solver incentive mechanisms on a live functional protocol. Where the governance token has exercisable utility on a live system and the protocol has no activated fee-distribution-to-holders mechanism, digital commodity classification is likely. Analysis turns on: (1) what governance actually controls; (2) whether any entity has disproportionate operational control over protocol parameters (central party question, n.54); (3) whether any fee-sharing with token holders exists or is governance-enabled. §III.A p.14; n.49; n.54 Revised
Perpetuals & derivatives DEXs
Native L1 / app-chain token; separate liquidity vault
Hyperliquid
HYPE: digital commodity
HLP vault: watch
 HYPE: native gas/staking token for Hyperliquid’s L1 perpetuals DEX — functional system, exercisable utility (gas, consensus staking), no apparent passive yield to holders. Likely digital commodity. Monitor: Hyperliquid Foundation’s concentrated development role and any operational control it retains (central party question, n.54). HLP vault is a distinct and harder question: vault depositors provide market-making liquidity and receive a share of trading P&L. Are vault depositors investing in a common enterprise with a reasonable expectation of profits from HLP’s market-making activities (managed or automated)? This raises the investment contract question independently of HYPE’s classification. §III.A p.14; §IV.A p.24–27; n.54 —

Privacy infrastructure
Governance tokens on functional privacy-preserving systems
Railgun (RAIL) Secret (SCRT) Aztec
Digital commodity
Non-securities risk distinct
 Governance tokens on functional privacy-preserving systems likely qualify as digital commodities under the taxonomy if they have exercisable utility and no passive yield. But the release explicitly preserves all other legal regimes (§VIII): “no interference is intended with respect to any other legal regime, including the Federal tax laws… or the Bank Secrecy Act of 1970 and the Anti-Money Laundering Act of 2020.” Classification as a non-security does not resolve the harder regulatory questions these protocols face: OFAC sanctions compliance, FinCEN/BSA obligations, and the interaction between privacy-preserving technology and financial surveillance obligations. These are potentially the more significant regulatory risks. §III.A p.14; §VIII p.63 (other legal regimes preserved) —
DeFi-native stablecoins
Crypto-collateralized; DAO governance; yield features
GHO (Aave) USDS (Sky) DAI (Maker)
Gray zone
Neither safe harbor applies
 Tier 1 (GENIUS Act) does not apply: these stablecoins are not issued by a bank subsidiary, federal QPSi, or state QPSi. Tier 2 (Covered Stablecoin) does not apply: crypto collateral, DAO governance, and/or yield features prevent qualification. On the yield point: the GENIUS Act’s prohibition on yield applies as a condition for being a “permitted payment stablecoin issuer” and obtaining Tier 1 status — it does not prohibit DeFi-native stablecoins from paying yield generally. USDS’s savings rate feature makes USDS ineligible for Tier 1; it does not make USDS statutorily prohibited. Yield-bearing wrappers (sUSDS, sDAI) do not qualify for the wrapping safe harbor (§VI) because the Wrapped Token Provider must not “directly or indirectly offer any return, yield, profit opportunity, or additional good or service” — these wrappers do exactly that. The release provides no specific interpretive guidance for DeFi-native stablecoins. §III.D p.21–23; GENIUS Act §§2(22), 4(a)(11), 17; §VI p.54–58 (wrapping conditions) Revised
